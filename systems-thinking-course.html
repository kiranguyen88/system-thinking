The Nature of Systems & The Shift in Thinking

3–4 hours Introductory

1.1 The Age of Complexity

The modern world faces unprecedented challenges: global climate crisis, pandemics, social inequality, and accelerating technological change. These problems cannot be solved with traditional linear thinking.

Core Assertion

“Complex problems cannot be solved with simple thinking tools” — We need a new way of seeing the world.

Real-World Example: Climate Change

Reducing CO₂ emissions is not just a technology issue. It involves:

  • Economic systems: Interests of energy corporations
  • Political systems: Electoral pressures and short-term policies
  • Social systems: Consumer habits and culture
  • Technological systems: Dependence on fossil fuels

A solution focusing on only one aspect will fail or create new problems.

Characteristics of Complex Problems

5 characteristics of “wicked problems”:

  1. Multi-causal: No single root cause
    • Example: Poverty is not just lack of money, but education, healthcare, infrastructure, policy, culture
  2. Interconnected: Elements influence each other
    • Example: Poor education → Low income → Poor health → Poor education (vicious cycle)
  3. Dynamic: Changes over time, not static
    • Example: Labor markets change faster than training programs
  4. Non-linear: Small changes → Big impacts (or vice versa)
    • Example: A single tweet can destroy a brand, or a million-dollar campaign can go unnoticed
  5. Delayed feedback: Results appear late
    • Example: Cutting education today → Consequences seen 10 years later

1.2 Defining a “System”

Definition

A system is a set of interacting and interdependent components that work together toward a common purpose.

Three elements of a system:

  1. Elements: Visible components (people, cells, products)
  2. Interconnections: Relationships between elements (information flows, material flows, rules)
  3. Function/Purpose: The goal the system is aiming for

Example: Education System

  • Elements: Students, teachers, textbooks, facilities
  • Interconnections: Schedules, teaching methods, evaluation systems, teacher-student relationships
  • Purpose: To develop competent and virtuous citizens

Distinction: System vs. Collection

SystemCollection
Interacting and interdependent partsIndependent, non-interacting parts
Has a common purposeNo common purpose
Changing one part affects the wholeChanging one part has little effect
Examples: Human body, company, ecosystemExamples: Pile of sand, books on a shelf, list of names

1.3 Linear vs. Non-linear Thinking

Linear ThinkingNon-linear (Systems) Thinking
A → B (simple cause)A ↔ B ↔ C (feedback loops)
Focus on isolated eventsFocus on patterns and structures
Solution: Treat symptomsSolution: Intervene in root structures
Result = Sum of partsResult > Sum of parts (emergence)
Timeframe: Short-term, immediateTimeframe: Along the system's lifecycle

Exercise: Distinguishing Thinking Styles

Situation: A company has a high employee turnover rate (30%/year).

Linear thinking would:

  1. Assume: “Employees are disloyal”
  2. Solutions: Raise salaries, add benefits, or hire new people
  3. Result: The problem persists

Systems thinking would:

  1. Ask: “What structure creates this pattern?”
  2. Analyze: Toxic culture? Poor management? No career path? Below-market pay?
  3. Solutions: Redesign management, evaluation, and development systems

1.4 Philosophical Foundation: Holism

Philosopher Jan Smuts (1926) defined: “The whole is not only greater than the sum of its parts, but also qualitatively different.”

Three characteristics of Holism:

  1. Emergence: The system has properties that individual parts do not
    • Example: “Team spirit” does not exist in one person, but emerges from interactions between members
    • Example: “Consciousness” is not in a single neuron, but emerges from a network of billions
  2. Context Matters: A part changes role depending on context
    • Example: A molecule in a leaf acts differently than in a root
    • Example: A person behaves differently at home, at work, and in a crowd
  3. Primacy of Relationships: Relationships are more important than individual elements
    • Example: In an ecosystem, survival depends on the food web, not just species traits
    • Example: In a company, culture and relationships matter more than individual stars

Practical Exercise

Analyze a familiar system:

  1. Choose a system you know well (family, company, school)
  2. List 5 key elements
  3. Describe 3 important relationships between them
  4. Identify the real purpose of the system (may differ from stated purpose)
  5. What would happen if you changed one relationship?

8 Skills & Mindsets of a Systems Thinker

4–5 hours Foundational

2.1 The 8 Core Skills

1. 10,000-foot Thinking

Seeing the big picture, stepping back from details to view the whole system

2. System as Cause

Recognizing that system structure drives behavior, not individual faults

3. Dynamic Thinking

Understanding systems change over time, not as static states

4. Operational Thinking

Identifying flows of materials, information, and decisions in the system

5. Feedback Loop Thinking

Differentiating reinforcing (growth/collapse) and balancing (stabilizing) loops

6. Non-linear Thinking

Accepting that small changes can have large impacts (leverage points)

7. Scientific Thinking

Testing assumptions, gathering data, and refining mental models

8. Empathy

Understanding the perspectives of different actors within the system

Details of Each Skill

1. 10,000-foot Thinking

Description: The ability to “fly high” to see the whole landscape.

Practice:

  • When facing a problem, pause and ask: “What is the bigger picture?”
  • Draw a system diagram before diving into details
  • Place the problem in a broader context (industry, society, global)

2. System as Cause

Description: Behavior comes from structure, not random events or individuals.

Practice:

  • Instead of asking “Who made the mistake?”, ask “What structure created this behavior?”
  • Analyze policies, processes, and incentives before blaming people

3. Dynamic Thinking

Description: Seeing the system as an ongoing process, not a snapshot.

Practice:

  • Draw trend lines over time
  • Ask: “When did this happen before? When will it happen again?”
  • Think in life cycles

4. Operational Thinking

Description: Understanding how the system actually works, not how it's described.

Practice:

  • Walk the actual process (go and see)
  • Draw a flowchart of the real process, not the theoretical one
  • Identify gaps between “say” and “do”

5. Feedback Loop Thinking

Description: Identifying circular cause-and-effect relationships.

Practice:

  • Draw Causal Loop Diagrams (CLDs)
  • Find reinforcing loops (growth/collapse)
  • Find balancing loops (stabilizing/constraining)

6. Non-linear Thinking

Description: Accepting that cause-effect relationships are not always proportional.

Practice:

  • Search for leverage points
  • Understand tipping points
  • Avoid oversimplification

7. Scientific Thinking

Description: Testing assumptions, gathering data, and updating models.

Practice:

  • Write down your assumptions
  • Find data to test them
  • Be willing to change when new evidence appears

8. Empathy

Description: Understanding the perspectives, emotions, and pressures of other actors.

Practice:

  • Create actor maps
  • Interview stakeholders
  • Put yourself in others' shoes

2.2 Metacognition

Metacognition is the ability to observe and adjust your own thinking models. It is the “meta” skill — thinking about thinking.

Three levels of metacognition:

  1. Awareness: “What thinking model am I using?”
  2. Evaluation: “Is this model effective in this context?”
  3. Adjustment: “Which model is more appropriate?”

Example: Shifting from Blame to System

Default thinking: “This employee is lazy, they need discipline.”

Metacognition: “My model is blaming the individual. Is there a system structure driving this behavior?”

Systems thinking: “Does the reward system encourage the desired behavior? Is the process clear?”

Reflective Journal (Writing is Thinking)

Spend 15 minutes each day writing about:

  • A problem you encountered
  • Your initial thinking model
  • Systems questions you could ask
  • An alternative thinking model

2.3 Empathy in Social Systems

In complex systems, actors have different perspectives, interests, and information. Empathy helps us understand why they act the way they do.

Three levels of systemic empathy:

  1. Emotional: Understanding others' feelings
  2. Cognitive: Understanding their perspective and beliefs
  3. Systemic: Understanding the pressures and constraints the system places on them

Core Insight

“Most people are not the problem — they are responding rationally to the system they are in.”

Example: Teacher and “Difficult” Student

Teacher's view: “This student is disruptive and refuses to learn.”

Student's view: “Everyone thinks I'm stupid, I can't keep up, better not to try.”

System view: Overloaded curriculum, no personalized support, grade pressure.

Systemic solution: Instead of punishing the student, redesign the learning support system.

Tool: Empathy Mapping

Empathy Map (4 quadrants):

Says

Direct quotes and statements

Thinks

Inner beliefs and thoughts (may not be spoken)

Does

Observable actions and behaviors

Feels

Emotions, anxieties, hopes

Practice: Choose a stakeholder, fill in the empathy map, then ask: “Why do they act this way within this system?”

System Language & Structure

4–5 hours Intermediate

3.1 Stocks & Flows Language

Stocks and Flows are the fundamental language for describing dynamic systems. Every system can be described by accumulations (stocks) and flows.

Definitions

Stock: An accumulation at a point in time — a noun (population, savings, inventory)

Flow: The rate of change of a stock — a verb (births/deaths, income/expenses, inbound/outbound)

Illustrative Example:

Real-world Applications:

  • Business: Cash (stock) ← Revenue (inflow) – Expenses (outflow)
  • Population: Population (stock) ← Births (inflow) – Deaths (outflow)
  • Inventory: Stock (stock) ← Receiving (inflow) – Shipping (outflow)

Exercise: Draw a Stocks & Flows Diagram

Choose a system and draw:

  1. Identify 2–3 main stocks (rectangles)
  2. Draw the flows in/out (arrows with valve ▶)
  3. Label units (people/year, $/month, products/day)
  4. Observe: Which stocks are increasing? Decreasing? Stable?

Common Stock & Flow Patterns

Pattern 1: Bathtub

Single stock with independent inflow and outflow. Examples: Bank account, water tank, inventory.

Pattern 2: Chain

Outflow of one stock is the inflow of the next. Example: Supply chain: Raw materials → Work-in-progress → Finished goods → Sales.

Pattern 3: Fork

One stock has multiple outflows. Example: Company budget → Marketing, R&D, Operations, HR.

Pattern 4: Convergence

Multiple inflows into one stock. Example: Revenue from multiple channels into company account.

3.2 Feedback Loops

Feedback loops are the self-regulating mechanisms of systems. Outputs return as inputs, influencing future behavior.

Reinforcing Loop (R)

Characteristic: Amplifies change, creating growth or collapse.

Symbol: R or (+)

Examples:

  • Compound interest: Money → Interest → More money → More interest
  • Social media: Likes → More visibility → More likes
  • Albedo effect: Ice melts → More heat absorbed → More melting

Balancing Loop (B)

Characteristic: Stabilizes the system, bringing it toward a goal.

Symbol: B or (–)

Examples:

  • Thermostat: High temp → Cooling on → Temp drops
  • Market: Supply > Demand → Price drops → Supply decreases
  • Body: Hunger → Eating → Fullness

Important Note

“Positive” does not mean good, “Negative” does not mean bad. Reinforcing loops can create growth (good) or collapse (bad). Balancing loops can stabilize (good) or constrain (bad).

Analysis: Systems with Multiple Loops

Most real systems have multiple interacting loops. Dominance (which loop prevails) can shift over time.

Example: Startup

  • Early stage: Reinforcing growth loop dominates (customers → revenue → marketing → customers)
  • Later stage: Balancing constraint loop dominates (market saturation, competition, rising costs)

Lesson: When loop dominance shifts, strategy must change. Continuing the old strategy will fail.

3.3 Delays and Counter-intuitiveness

Delays:

In systems, effects often do not happen immediately. Delays between action and outcome can lead to:

  • Overshooting: Acting too much because results aren't seen immediately
  • Oscillation: Fluctuating around a target
  • Overcorrection: Adjusting too far

Example: Shower Temperature

You turn the hot water knob → It doesn't feel warm right away → Turn it more → Water gets too hot → Turn it back → Too cold → ...

Lesson: The delay between turning the knob and feeling the temperature creates oscillation. Solution: Turn slowly and wait.

Why do obvious solutions often make problems worse?

  1. Focusing on symptoms: Solving events, not structures
  2. Ignoring delays: Acting before seeing results
  3. Familiar with reinforcing loops: Not recognizing that balancing loops will backfire
  4. Optimizing parts: Optimizing one part harms the whole system

Example: Traffic Congestion and Road Expansion

Obvious solution: Traffic jam → Widen the road.

Actual result: Wider road → More people drive → Congestion returns (induced demand).

Systemic solution: Develop public transport, mixed-use urban planning, remote work.

Simulation Game: Beer Distribution Game

Description: Simulates the beer supply chain from Factory → Distributor → Wholesaler → Retailer → Customer.

Rules: Each role only sees orders from the next tier, not the whole system.

Typical result: Oscillation and the Bullwhip Effect — volatility increases from customers up to the factory.

Lessons:

  • Delays create overshooting
  • Lack of system-wide information → Wrong decisions
  • Blaming others → It's actually the system structure

Tools to Visualize Invisible Structures

5–6 hours Intermediate

4.1 The Iceberg Model

The Iceberg Model is a powerful tool to move from surface events down to deeper structures and root beliefs.

EventsAbove water (10%)

Question: What is happening? Specific incidents, daily situations — what we react to immediately.
Intervention: Reactive

Waterline

PatternsJust below the surface (20%)

Question: What repeats over time? Trends, cycles, recurring behaviors — allows prediction.
Intervention: Anticipate

StructuresDeeper (30%)

Question: What structures create these patterns? Rules, policies, processes, reward systems, power distribution.
Intervention: Design

Mental modelsDeepest (40%)

Question: What beliefs/assumptions hold these structures in place? Values, beliefs, worldviews, assumptions about how the world works.
Intervention: Transform

Example: Employee Turnover

  • Events: “Two top employees resigned today.”
  • Patterns: “8 top employees have resigned in the last 3 months, turnover rate is 25%.”
  • Structures: “Performance reviews are based only on short-term KPIs, no career development, below-market pay.”
  • Mental Models: “Employees are a cost, not an asset”; “Paying minimum wage is enough because they have no options.”

Solutions at each level:

  • Events: Hire new people (temporary)
  • Patterns: Employee surveys, salary benchmarking
  • Structures: Redesign performance system, create development paths
  • Mental Models: Shift leadership beliefs about human value

Exercise: Analyze a Problem Using the Iceberg

Choose a current problem you face:

  1. Events: Describe the most recent specific incident
  2. Patterns: How often has this happened? When? With whom?
  3. Structures: What rules, processes, or systems create this pattern?
  4. Mental Models: What beliefs/assumptions hold this structure in place?
  5. Intervention: At which level can you act? Which level offers the highest leverage?

4.2 Actor Mapping

Actor Mapping helps identify stakeholders, power relationships, and influence flows within a social system.

Steps:

  1. Define the topic: What is the problem or system goal?
  2. Draw the system boundary: Draw a large circle (system) and a small inner circle (core interest).
  3. List actors: Who influences or is affected by the issue?
  4. Place on the map: Position based on influence (closer to center = higher influence).
  5. Draw connections: Arrows showing influence, collaboration, or conflict.
  6. Analyze: Which clusters exist? Where are blockages? What are leverage points?

Example: Local Education System

Key actors:

  • Center: Students, Parents
  • Inner ring: Teachers, Principals, District education office
  • Outer ring: Provincial education dept, local government, businesses, NGOs

Important connections:

  • Province → District → School → Teacher → Student (top-down power)
  • Parents ↔ Teachers (two-way feedback)
  • Businesses → Schools (hiring, sponsorship)

Template: Actor Mapping Canvas

Guiding Questions:

  • Who are the primary beneficiaries?
  • Who holds power/makes decisions?
  • Who has critical information?
  • Who has resources (money, people, influence)?
  • Who is negatively affected?
  • Who can block or support?

Symbols:

  • → : One-way influence
  • ↔ : Collaboration/Two-way
  • ⚡ : Conflict
  • 💰 : Money flow
  • 📊 : Information flow

4.3 Ecocycle Mapping

Ecocycle Mapping, based on adaptive cycle theory, helps understand the stages of development, conservation, creative destruction, and renewal in organizations/projects.

4 Stages of the Ecocycle

r: Exploitation

Startup, rapid growth, new opportunities, experimentation

K: Conservation

Stability, high efficiency, resource accumulation, but rigidity

Ω: Release

Crisis, resource liberation, breaking old structures

α: Reorganization

Renewal, reinvestment, start of a new cycle

Applications:

  • Diagnosis: What stage is the organization/project in?
  • Strategy: Actions appropriate to the stage
  • Portfolio: Balance projects across different stages

Example: Startup Company

  • r (2018–2020): 200% annual growth, experimenting with many models
  • K (2021–2023): Stabilizing, optimizing, process-driven, but losing creativity
  • Ω (2024): Pandemic, market crash, 40% workforce reduction
  • α (2025–): Focus on core product, new business model, new culture

Ecocycle Matrix: Questions for Each Stage

r (Exploitation)What opportunities are untapped? What to experiment with?Invest, experiment, scale fast
K (Conservation)How to optimize? How to sustain?Processize, accumulate, protect
Ω (Release)What to let go? What to keep?Cut, liberate, learn
α (Reorganization)What new can be built? What lessons learned?Innovate, reinvest, restart

System Reasoning & Collective Modeling

4–5 hours Advanced

5.1 Collective System Reasoning

Instead of giving personal opinions, build proposals based on solid, linked, and transparent reasoning.

Structure of a system argument:

  1. Observation: “What data/events do we see?”
  2. Analysis: “What patterns/structures create this?”
  3. Assumptions: “What assumptions are we using?”
  4. Conclusion: “So, what should we do?”

Principle

“Separate observation from interpretation.”

Skill: Ladder of Inference

The Ladder of Inference helps reveal how we move from data to conclusions.

Practice: In a conflict, “go down the ladder” — ask: “What data did you see? I saw different data…”

5.2 Structuring Inquiry

Asking the right questions to explore the system rather than giving premature answers.

Types of system questions:

  • About elements: “Who/what are the components of this system?”
  • About connections: “How do the components interact?”
  • About purpose: “What purpose is this system truly serving?”
  • About time: “When did this happen before? When will it happen again?”
  • About perspective: “How do others in the system see it?”

The 5 Whys

The 5 Whys technique asks “Why” five times to move from symptom to root cause.

Example: Machine Stopped

  1. Why did the machine stop? → Overload, fuse blew
  2. Why was it overloaded? → Bearing lacked lubrication
  3. Why was lubrication insufficient? → Oil pump wasn't working
  4. Why wasn't the pump working? → Pump shaft was worn
  5. Why was the shaft worn? → No filter, dust got in → Root cause!

Solution: Install a filter, not keep replacing fuses.

5.3 Top-Down Elaboration (TDE)

A tool to summarize a project/problem on a single page to create conceptual alignment.

TDE Structure (1 page)

  1. Title: Project/problem name
  2. Context: Why does this matter?
  3. Purpose: What is the ultimate goal?
  4. Scope: What's in/out of the system?
  5. Key stakeholders: Who matters?
  6. Core structure: 3–5 key elements
  7. Risks/Assumptions: What could go wrong?
  8. Next steps: Immediate actions

5.4 Modeling Together

Using diagramming as a conversation to surface hidden assumptions and create shared understanding.

Process:

  1. Prepare: Whiteboard, large paper, sticky notes, markers
  2. Start: One person draws the first element
  3. Build: Each person adds an element/connection
  4. Challenge: “Is this accurate? What's missing?”
  5. Finalize: Agree on the common version

Benefits

  • Surface different assumptions
  • Create a common language
  • Identify gaps in understanding
  • Increase ownership and commitment

Facilitation Tips

For an effective session:

  • Psychological safety: No “wrong” ideas, only “incomplete” ones
  • Equal voice: Invite quieter people first, leaders speak later
  • Visual first: Draw before discussing — avoid invisible debates
  • Time box: Limit each step (10–15 minutes)
  • Document: Take photos, notes, share afterward

Facilitator questions:

  • “What's missing?”
  • “What's not right?”
  • “How do others see it?”
  • “What is the story here?”

Practical Application & Systems Leadership

5–6 hours Advanced

6.1 Finding Leverage Points

Leverage points are places where a small change can create a large, lasting impact on the entire system. Donella Meadows (1999) identified 12 leverage points, from low to high:

Low Leverage (Visible, less effective)

  1. Constants, parameters (numbers, standards)
  2. Buffers (reservoirs)
  3. Stock-and-flow structures (physical layout)
  4. Delays
  5. Balancing loops
  6. Reinforcing loops

High Leverage (Hidden, highly effective)

  1. Information flows
  2. Rules (incentives, constraints)
  3. Self-organization
  4. Goals (system purpose)
  5. Paradigms (mindset)
  6. Transcending paradigms

Example: Healthcare System

Low leverage: Increase hospital beds, buy new equipment (expensive, short-term).

Medium leverage: Change patient flow processes, doctor incentives.

High leverage: Shift the goal from “treating sickness” to “preventing sickness”; Shift the paradigm from “patients are a burden” to “health is an asset.”

6.2 Systems Leadership

Systems leaders don't control; they coordinate, nurture the learning ecosystem, and facilitate the flow of knowledge.

5 characteristics of a Systems Leader:

  1. Hold the big picture: See the whole system, not just the parts
  2. Listen across dimensions: Understand multiple actor perspectives
  3. Ask powerful questions: Provoke thought, not provide answers
  4. Embrace uncertainty: Comfortable with ambiguity, not needing to control everything
  5. Facilitate learning: Create safe spaces for experimentation and learning

Role Shift

From: Hero leader (solving all problems)
To: Host leader (creating space for others to solve)

5 Practices of a Systems Leader

1. See the System

Identify patterns, structures, mental models

2. Facilitate Reflection

Create space for the team to reflect and learn

3. Shift from Blame to Learning

Change the culture from blaming to learning

4. Build Shared Understanding

Use tools to create a common language

5. Catalyze Collective Action

Connect stakeholders to act together

6.3 Designing for Resilience

Resilience is the ability of a system to recover and learn from failure, rather than collapsing under shock.

3 elements of Resilience:

  1. Diversity: Multiple ways to respond, not dependent on a single solution
  2. Modularity: Independent parts, failure doesn't spread through the whole system
  3. Feedback: Fast information to adjust in time

Example: Project Portfolio

Low resilience: 100% budget in 1 big project → If it fails, the company collapses.

High resilience: 70% in stable projects, 20% in growth projects, 10% in experiments → If experiments fail, the company survives.

Checklist: Designing a Resilient System

Diversity:

  • ☐ Are there multiple revenue sources?
  • ☐ Are there diverse skills in the team?
  • ☐ Are there multiple scenario plans?

Modularity:

  • ☐ Can teams operate independently?
  • ☐ Can a failure in one part be isolated?
  • ☐ Is there redundancy?

Feedback:

  • ☐ Are metrics real-time?
  • ☐ Are there fast feedback loops?
  • ☐ Is there a culture of learning from failure?

6.4 Redefining Success

Instead of measuring success by optimizing individual parts, measure by the sustainable development of the entire system.

System success indicators:

  • Sustainability: Can the system last long-term?
  • Equity: Are benefits distributed fairly?
  • Adaptability: Can the system adapt to change?
  • Learning: Does the system learn and improve?
  • Well-being: Are the actors within the system thriving?

Example: Company

Old success: Quarterly profit, stock price, market share.

New success: Employee growth, customer loyalty, positive social impact, adaptability, continuous learning.

Teaching methods

How the course is meant to be run: learners write, play, draw and apply every tool to a real problem of their own.

1. Writing is Thinking

Encourage learners to keep a reflective journal to untangle complex thoughts. Writing forces clarity, reveals gaps, and connects ideas.

  • Daily journal: 15 minutes on a system problem
  • Write explanations for others to understand
  • Draw diagrams with captions

2. Discovery Learning

Use simulation games (like the Beer Game) for learners to discover system structure lessons themselves rather than being taught theory.

  • Beer Distribution Game: Experience delays and oscillation in supply chains
  • Fishbanks: Experience the tragedy of the commons
  • Simulation: Model feedback loops

3. Visual Modeling

Practice drawing diagrams continuously on whiteboards or software to turn abstract thinking into tangible reality.

  • Causal Loop Diagrams (CLD)
  • Stock and Flow Diagrams
  • Iceberg Model worksheets
  • Actor Maps on large paper

4. Project-Based Learning

Each learner applies the tools to a real problem they face, reporting progress throughout the sessions.
